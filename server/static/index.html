What is a trebuchet?

A trebuchet (say "TREB-yoo-shay") is a throwing machine from the Middle Ages. The first ones were pulled by teams of people; from the 1100s, builders swapped the people for a heavy box of stones or earth, the counterweight trebuchet. The biggest could throw stones heavier than a person a few hundred metres, and stayed in use until gunpowder took over.

A trebuchet is not a catapult in the strict sense: a catapult stores energy by bending or twisting something (a spring, a rope), while a trebuchet uses gravity: a weight that falls.

How it works

  1. Loaded: the arm is held down by the trigger. The counterweight is high up, storing energy (potential energy).
  2. Fire: pull the trigger pin. The weight falls and the arm swings up.
  3. Drag: the sling pulls the projectile along the trough, then lifts it.
  4. Whip: the sling swings round the end of the arm, going faster than the arm itself, like the end of a whip.
  5. Release: the ring slides off the bent pin at the arm's tip and the projectile flies off (kinetic energy).

The parts of your trebuchet

Frames (2)
The A-shaped sides. Each holds a skateboard bearing that the axle turns in.
Cross members (3) and trough
Join the frames at table level. The projectile and sling lie on the trough; the trigger post is on it too.
Arm
The long throwing stick. A hole for the axle, a hinge hole at the short end, a cord hole and the release-pin hole at the tip.
Bucket and lids
Holds the counterweight fill. It hangs from the short end of the arm on a bolt, so it swings freely.
Axle spacers (2)
Keep the arm in the middle of the axle.
Trigger pin
Holds the arm down through a cord loop. Pull it to fire.
Pin gauge
A wedge cut to the suggested release angle, for bending the release pin.
Ball
A printable projectile. Print a few: they get lost.

Besides the printed parts you need an M8 bolt, two 608 bearings, an M4 bolt, about 10 small screws, string, a scrap of leather, a small ring, a paper clip and the counterweight fill. The report's What else you need lists exact sizes for your design, with links.

Printing tips

  • PLA is fine. PLA+ or PETG is tougher for the arm.
  • Every part is exported lying the way it should print, with no supports needed.
  • The arm and frames take the most force: 4 walls, 30–40% infill. Everything else: 2–3 walls, 15–20% infill.
  • The biggest parts are the frames and the trough. Step 2 checks they fit your bed; a longer arm needs a bigger printer.
  • Clean any stringy plastic out of the holes before you put it together. The bearings should press in firmly.

What makes it throw farther

Counterweight
The energy comes from the weight falling. Double the weight, roughly double the energy. Very heavy weights strain the parts.
Weight ratio
Counterweight ÷ projectile. Above about 100 : 1 the arm can't swing much faster, so extra weight helps less and less.
Arm ratio
Long side ÷ short side. Around 3.5–4.5 : 1 usually works best for a hinged counterweight.
Sling length
A longer sling acts like a longer arm, but too long and it drags. Try 0.7–1.0 × the long side.
Release angle
When the ring slides off the pin. The report suggests the best angle; the pin gauge helps you bend it.
Friction
Bearings on the axle and a smooth trough help. A loose, wobbly machine wastes energy.

Tuning and troubleshooting

What happensWhat to try
The ball goes straight up or backwardsIt lets go too early. Bend the release pin straighter (smaller angle).
The ball slams into the table or floor in frontIt lets go too late. Bend the pin more (bigger angle).
The ball doesn't leave the pouchThe ring is stuck: smooth the pin, use a smaller ring, or bend the pin a little more.
The arm swings slowly or stopsToo much friction: loosen the axle nut slightly; check the bearings are seated and the spacers aren't rubbing.
The bucket hits somethingCheck the hinge bolt is snug but the bucket swings freely; nothing should be inside the frames below the arm.
Throws vary a lotLay the sling straight and flat every time, and use the same projectile. Take the middle of five throws.
A part crackedReprint it with more walls and infill (or PETG), or use less counterweight.

Experiments to try

  • Counterweight: throw with 200, 300 and 400 g. Does double the weight throw twice as far?
  • Sling length: try a short, medium and long sling. Which works best, and does it match the report's suggestion?
  • Release angle: bend the pin in 10° steps and record where it lands. Compare with the release-angle chart.
  • Projectiles: a marble against a ping-pong ball. Which goes farther, and why? (Hint: air drag.)
  • Real against model: measure five throws from the axle, take the middle one, and see what percentage of the model's distance you get.

About the estimate

The page simulates the swing: the falling counterweight, the arm, the sling dragging the ball along the trough, the ball swinging free, the release and the flight with air drag. It doesn't model friction, stretchy string or a bendy arm, so real throws land shorter. The report shows the model's number and the range to expect (60–85% of it).

Distances are measured from the axle, landing at table height. Efficiency is how much of the weight's falling energy ends up in the projectile.

Key terms

Axle (pivot)
The bolt the arm swings on, held by the two frames.
Hinged counterweight
The weight hangs from a hinge instead of being fixed to the arm. It falls more straight down, so more of its energy reaches the projectile.
Trough
The flat track the projectile slides along before the sling lifts it.
Release pin
A short bent pin at the tip of the arm. One end of the sling has a ring that slides off it to let go.
Release angle
The angle between the sling and the arm when the ring slides off: set by how far the pin is bent.
Trigger
A pin and cord loop that hold the arm down until you pull it.
Potential energy
Energy stored by lifting something up. The raised counterweight has it.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion. The flying projectile has it.
Efficiency
Kinetic energy of the projectile ÷ potential energy the weight gave up. Good trebuchets reach 60–75%.
Launch angle
How steeply the projectile leaves. Without air, 45° goes farthest; with air, a bit lower.

Safety

  • Grown-up supervision. Safety glasses for everyone nearby.
  • Never aim at people, animals, windows or anything breakable.
  • Keep hands and faces out of the arm's swing; it swings hard and fast.
  • Load it with the trigger in place, and only when everyone is behind the machine.
  • Soft or light things indoors; marbles only outdoors with a clear field.
