Quick start

  1. Design. Work through the numbered steps on the left. The estimated throw under Build updates as you go.
  2. Build. Press Build: the 3D model and a full report appear. Press ▶ Throw to watch it swing, or open the test throw to see it to scale.
  3. Print. Download the ZIP: the parts to print, a shopping list, the build steps and your design file.
  4. Throw. Snap it together, tune the release angle and measure your throws. Compare them with the estimate.

Beginner shows the six steps that matter most. Advanced adds the arm ratio, release angle, start angle, bucket and arm sizes, axle type and infill. Drag the handles between the panels to resize them (double-click a handle to reset it).

What is a trebuchet?

A trebuchet (say "TREB-yoo-shay") is a throwing machine from the Middle Ages. The first ones were pulled by teams of people; from the 1100s, builders swapped the people for a heavy box of stones or earth, the counterweight trebuchet. The biggest could throw stones heavier than a person a few hundred metres, and stayed in use until gunpowder took over.

A trebuchet is not a catapult in the strict sense: a catapult stores energy by bending or twisting something (a spring, a rope), while a trebuchet uses gravity: a weight that falls.

How it works

  1. Loaded: the arm is held down by the trigger. The counterweight is high up, storing energy (potential energy).
  2. Fire: pull the trigger pin. The weight falls and the arm swings up.
  3. Drag: the sling pulls the projectile along the trough, then lifts it.
  4. Whip: the sling swings round the end of the arm, going faster than the arm itself, like the end of a whip.
  5. Release: the ring slides off the hook at the arm's tip and the projectile flies off (kinetic energy).

The parts of your trebuchet

Frames (2, the same part)
The sides: a rail on the table and a tower holding the axle. The trigger hole is beside the axle; notches under the rail measure the sling cords.
Cross members (2) and deck
The members' split ends click through slots in both frames. The deck drops onto their pegs; the projectile and pouch lie on it.
Arm
The long throwing stick: axle hole, hinge hole at the short end, trigger hole near the axle, and the release hook and a cord hole at the tip.
Bucket and cap
Holds the counterweight (coins go in through the hole in the top). It hangs from the short end of the arm, so it swings freely.
Axle and hinge pins
Printed pins: push them through and the split end clicks behind the far side. Spacers and sleeves keep the arm in the middle.
Trigger pin
Goes through the frame into the arm and holds it down. Pull it (with a cord) to fire.
Sling pouch and ring
Two cords make the sling: the pouch cradles the ball, the ring slides off the release hook.
Ball
A printable projectile. Print a few: they get lost.

Everything prints on one plate and snaps together: no screws, bolts or glue. Besides the printed parts you need string for the sling and trigger, and the counterweight (coins work). An M8 bolt and two 608 skateboard bearings are an option for a smoother axle. The report's What else you need lists it for your design, with links.

Printing tips

  • PLA is fine. PLA+ or PETG is tougher for the arm.
  • The ZIP has the whole kit laid out on one plate (plate_1.3mf), each part lying the way it should print, with no supports needed.
  • The arm and frames take the most force: 4 walls, 30–40% infill. Print the pins solid (100%). Everything else: 2–3 walls, 15–20% infill.
  • A bigger arm or more counterweight can spill onto a second plate; the report says so.
  • Clean any stringy plastic out of the holes and slots. If the snaps are too tight or loose for your printer, change Clearance (Advanced) and build again.

What makes it throw farther

Counterweight
The energy comes from the weight falling. Double the weight, roughly double the energy. Very heavy weights strain the parts.
Weight ratio
Counterweight ÷ projectile. Above about 100 : 1 the arm can't swing much faster, so extra weight helps less and less.
Arm ratio
Long side ÷ short side. Around 3.5–4.5 : 1 usually works best for a hinged counterweight.
Sling length
A longer sling acts like a longer arm, but too long and it drags. Try 0.7–1.0 × the long side.
Release angle
When the ring slides off the hook. The report suggests the best angle, and the arm's hook is printed at it.
Friction
A smooth axle and deck help; the bearing option is smoothest. A loose, wobbly machine wastes energy.

Tuning and troubleshooting

What happensWhat to try
The ball goes straight up or backwardsIt lets go too early. Set a smaller release angle (5° at a time) and reprint the arm.
The ball slams into the table or floor in frontIt lets go too late. Set a bigger release angle and reprint the arm.
The ball doesn't leave the pouchThe ring is catching: smooth the hook with a file, or use a small metal split ring instead.
The arm swings slowly or stopsToo much friction: clean the holes, check the spacers aren't rubbing, or try a little more Clearance.
A snap is too tight or falls outChange Clearance (Advanced) by 0.1 mm and reprint that part.
Throws vary a lotLay the sling straight and flat every time, and use the same projectile. Take the middle of five throws.
A part crackedReprint it with more walls and infill (or PETG), or use less counterweight.

Experiments to try

  • Counterweight: throw with 200, 300 and 400 g. Does double the weight throw twice as far?
  • Sling length: try a short, medium and long sling. Which works best, and does it match the report's suggestion?
  • Release angle: print arms 10° apart and record where each lands. Compare with the release-angle chart.
  • Projectiles: a marble against a ping-pong ball. Which goes farther, and why? (Hint: air drag.)
  • Real against model: measure five throws from the axle, take the middle one, and see what percentage of the model's distance you get.

About the estimate

The page simulates the swing: the falling counterweight, the arm, the sling dragging the ball along the trough, the ball swinging free, the release and the flight with air drag. It doesn't model friction, stretchy string or a bendy arm, so real throws land shorter. The report shows the model's number and the range to expect (60–85% of it).

Distances are measured from the axle, landing at table height. Efficiency is how much of the weight's falling energy ends up in the projectile.

Key terms

Axle (pivot)
The pin the arm swings on, held by the two frames.
Hinged counterweight
The weight hangs from a hinge instead of being fixed to the arm. It falls more straight down, so more of its energy reaches the projectile.
Trough
The flat track the projectile slides along before the sling lifts it.
Release hook
A short prong at the tip of the arm. One end of the sling has a ring that slides off it to let go.
Release angle
The angle between the sling and the arm when the ring slides off: set by the hook's angle.
Trigger
A pin through the frame into the arm that holds it down until you pull it.
Potential energy
Energy stored by lifting something up. The raised counterweight has it.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion. The flying projectile has it.
Efficiency
Kinetic energy of the projectile ÷ potential energy the weight gave up. Good trebuchets reach 60–75%.
Launch angle
How steeply the projectile leaves. Without air, 45° goes farthest; with air, a bit lower.

Safety

  • Grown-up supervision. Safety glasses for everyone nearby.
  • Never aim at people, animals, windows or anything breakable.
  • Keep hands and faces out of the arm's swing; it swings hard and fast.
  • Load it with the trigger in place, and only when everyone is behind the machine.
  • Soft or light things indoors; marbles only outdoors with a clear field.
